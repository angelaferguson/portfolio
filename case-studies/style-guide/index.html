    Building and governing a team style guide

    Ten Years, one style guide, two different problems to solve
    Team size 20-25 writers
    My role Style Guide creator & lead
    Scope 50–150 entries

    Tags: Content strategy · Style guide · Team leadership · Process improvement

    The problem

    When I joined the team, there was no shared style reference. Writers resolved ambiguous decisions — how to style a UI element, whether to spell out a number, what voice to use in a warning — by digging through past deliverables to find a precedent. It was slow, and it still produced inconsistent results.

    Peer review was where this really showed up. When you edited someone's work, you were often working from instinct rather than a documented rule. That made it harder to give confident feedback, and harder for writers to receive it — because without a shared reference, an edit can feel like a matter of opinion rather than a question of consistency. Having somewhere to point makes a real difference.

    Building from scratch

    I researched industry conventions and drew on established references to build a defensible baseline, covering terminology, formatting, UI element naming, voice and tone, punctuation, numbers and dates, abbreviations, and product-specific terms. Once a solid draft existed, my co-lead and I ran structured discussions with the full writing team to introduce guidelines, surface disagreements, and build buy-in.

    That last part mattered as much as the writing itself. A style guide only works if the team trusts it — and trust comes from being part of the conversation, not just being handed a document. Over time, peer review got easier. Writers spent less time hunting for precedents, new team members had a reference point from their first week, and reviewers could point to a specific guideline instead of defending a judgment call.

    Governance: the harder problem

    After several years, ownership of the style guide changed hands. When I returned to leading it, I inherited a document that had grown significantly — but not cleanly. Guidelines contradicted each other. Examples were meant to illustrate specific rules but often illustrated something else, or contained errors that quietly violated other rules. Some sections had expanded into long, discursive passages that read more like thinking-out-loud than usable reference entries.

    This is a governance problem, and it's a common one. Style guides don't fail because the original rules were wrong — they drift when changes accumulate without a consistent voice or a clear standard for what a good entry looks like. The result is a document writers stop trusting.

    My current work is a systematic audit: resolving contradictions (treating each one as a decision that needs team input, not just a quick fix), rewriting examples that don't accurately illustrate their guidelines, restructuring entries that are hard to scan, and removing content that doesn't belong in a style guide at all. I'm involving the team throughout — both to validate decisions and to rebuild confidence in the guide as a reliable source of truth.
